## Pamamanhikan (formal proposal visit)

**Tags:** pamamanhikan, proposal, family-introduction, traditions, pre-wedding
**Applies to:** all couples (traditional Filipino practice; modern interpretations vary)
**Cross-ref:** 02_Regional_Pricing_Benchmarks.md (gifts/food costs), wedding-planning timeline
**Source:** Filipino cultural practice · CBCP family handbook · Setnayan internal observations
**Last verified:** 2026-05-18 · Setnayan team

Pamamanhikan is the formal visit by the groom's family to the bride's family to ask for her hand in marriage. It's typically a meal at the bride's family home with both immediate families present. Sometimes the engaged couple plans details (wedding date, venue preferences, financial responsibilities) collaboratively at this gathering; sometimes it's purely ceremonial.

In modern Filipino practice, the pamamanhikan often happens after the couple has already decided to marry — it's the families' first formal meeting rather than the proposal moment itself. The groom's family typically brings food (kakanin, a roast pig, fruits) as a gesture of respect. Both families may discuss the wedding date, who pays for what, and who'll be invited.

Some couples skip pamamanhikan entirely — particularly second marriages, civil unions, or couples whose families have already met casually. There's no universal rule; the practice varies by region (more common in Visayas/Mindanao than in NCR) and by family tradition.

### Common follow-ups
- "Do we have to do pamamanhikan?"
- "Who pays for the pamamanhikan meal?"
- "What if our families don't get along?"

### Caveats / what NOT to say
- Don't insist on pamamanhikan as required — many modern Filipino couples skip it.
- Don't prescribe specific gifts — they vary by family preference.

## Ninang and Ninong (principal + secondary sponsors)

**Tags:** ninang, ninong, sponsors, principal, secondary, catholic, civil
**Applies to:** Catholic + Civil weddings; Muslim weddings have different roles
**Cross-ref:** 0001 guest list (role taxonomy), 04_Planning_Timelines.md (sponsor lock timing)
**Source:** CBCP marriage handbook · Catholic Bishops' Conference of the Philippines guidance
**Last verified:** 2026-05-18 · Setnayan team

Ninang (godmother) and ninong (godfather) sponsors play a meaningful role in Filipino weddings. **Principal sponsors** are typically older married couples the engaged couple looks up to as mentors — their role is symbolic witnesses to the marriage and life-long supporters of the couple. Catholic weddings usually have 2-6 principal sponsor couples (parish requirements vary).

**Secondary sponsors** handle specific ritual duties during the Catholic ceremony: the **candle sponsors** light unity candles, the **cord sponsors** place a decorative cord on the couple symbolizing union, the **veil sponsors** drape a veil over the couple representing protection, and the **coin sponsors** present the arras (13 coins symbolizing financial provision and shared resources). Each role is typically held by one couple, though some weddings have multiple secondary sponsor couples per role.

For Civil weddings, sponsors are optional — the couple needs at least 2 witnesses of legal age, who can be anyone (sometimes the same people serve as both witnesses and sponsors). For Muslim weddings, the wali (the bride's male guardian) and witnesses fulfill the structural role.

**Plan ahead.** Sponsors' names go on the invitations, so confirm them 8-10 months before the wedding (per 04_Planning_Timelines.md). Asking someone to be a sponsor is a meaningful honor — give them time to decline gracefully if they can't commit.

### Common follow-ups
- "How many principal sponsors should we have?"
- "Can we have unmarried friends as sponsors?"
- "Do sponsors have to attend the ceremony?"

### Caveats / what NOT to say
- Don't prescribe a fixed number — Catholic parishes vary; some allow only 1 principal couple, others welcome 6.
- Don't insist on married sponsors — modern practice accepts unmarried friends as primary witnesses in Civil ceremonies.

## Candle, cord, and veil ceremony

**Tags:** candle, cord, veil, secondary-sponsors, catholic, ceremony, unity-candle
**Applies to:** Catholic weddings (Civil + Muslim weddings have different rituals)
**Cross-ref:** 04_Planning_Timelines.md
**Source:** CBCP marriage rite manual
**Last verified:** 2026-05-18 · Setnayan team

During a Catholic Filipino wedding, three symbolic rituals occur after the vows:

- **Candle ceremony** — two candle sponsors (typically a married couple) light two side candles, then the bride and groom together light a single central unity candle. Symbolizes two becoming one.
- **Cord ceremony** — cord sponsors drape a decorative cord (yugal) over both the bride and groom in a figure-8 pattern. Symbolizes the eternal bond of marriage.
- **Veil ceremony** — veil sponsors place a long veil over both partners. Symbolizes the couple's mutual care and the protection of God over the marriage.

Some weddings include a **coin ceremony (arras)** — the groom hands the bride 13 coins (the arras) as a symbol of his commitment to provide. The bride receives and returns them as a symbol of their shared financial life.

These rituals are typically conducted by **secondary sponsor couples** (different couples for each ritual is most common; some weddings have one secondary couple do multiple rituals). Confirm with your parish priest which rituals are standard for that diocese — practices vary.

### Common follow-ups
- "Do we have to do all four rituals?"
- "Can we add our own rituals?"
- "Who provides the candles, cord, and veil?"

### Caveats / what NOT to say
- Don't insist on rigid order — varies by parish.
- Civil weddings don't include these rituals.

## Catholic, Civil, and Muslim wedding tracks

**Tags:** catholic, civil, muslim, religious-tradition, ceremony-type
**Applies to:** all couples (intake answer routes the wizard)
**Cross-ref:** 05_Legal_BIR_Reference.md, 04_Planning_Timelines.md (Pre-Cana timing)
**Source:** PSA · LCR · CBCP · Muslim Personal Code P.D. 1083
**Last verified:** 2026-05-18 · Setnayan team

Three primary ceremony tracks in Filipino weddings:

**Catholic wedding** — requires Pre-Cana seminar (60-90 day parish notice), canonical interview, full document load (CENOMAR · baptismal cert · confirmation cert · banns of marriage in some dioceses), and a Catholic celebrant officiating in an approved venue (typically a Catholic church). Ceremony usually 60-90 minutes; includes unity candle / cord / veil / arras rituals. Most common in NCR / Visayas / Mindanao Catholic families.

**Civil wedding** — performed by a judge, mayor, or authorized solemnizing officer at the Local Civil Registry (LCR) or any approved civil venue. Requires marriage license (4-month application window, 120-day validity), 2 witnesses of legal age, optional family + close friends in attendance. Ceremony usually 20-40 minutes. Simpler legal load; no religious requirements.

**Muslim wedding** — governed by Muslim Personal Code (Presidential Decree 1083). The Islamic wedding (nikah) includes the offer (ijab) and acceptance (qabul), a marital dowry (mahr) from groom to bride, the bride's wali (male guardian) consent, and two adult Muslim male witnesses. Performed by an imam or qadi. Filipino Muslims may also have a separate Civil wedding for state recognition; many do both.

Some Filipino couples have **combined ceremonies** — e.g., Catholic + Civil (Civil for legal recognition + Catholic for family). The legal requirements stack; check with both authorities.

### Common follow-ups
- "Can we just have a Civil wedding?"
- "Do we have to do both Catholic and Civil?"
- "What if I'm Catholic and my partner is Muslim?"

### Caveats / what NOT to say
- Don't prescribe interfaith solutions — they vary by family and faith leader.
- Don't dismiss Muslim Personal Code as secondary — it's the legal framework for Filipino Muslims.

## The Nikah — Islamic marriage contract

**Tags:** nikah, muslim, islamic, ceremony-type, ijab, qabul, validity
**Applies to:** Muslim couples (`ceremony_type='muslim'`)
**Cross-ref:** Muslim_Wedding_Spec_2026-06-28.md, 05_Legal_BIR_Reference.md, 04_Planning_Timelines.md
**Source:** Code of Muslim Personal Laws (PD 1083) · Qur'an 4:4 · NCMF · ITQAN Academy Muslim Marriage Rules
**Last verified:** 2026-06-28 · Setnayan team (pending imam review)

A valid Islamic marriage (nikah) rests on five essentials: the free **consent** of both bride and groom; the **wali** (the bride's male guardian) giving consent and representing her; at least **two adult Muslim witnesses**; the **mahr** (the groom's mandatory gift to the bride, hers alone); and the **contract itself** — the offer (ijab) and acceptance (qabul) stated in one sitting and solemnized by an imam or qadi. In the Philippines this is governed by the Code of Muslim Personal Laws (PD 1083); a marriage solemnized by Muslim rites and registered with the Shari'a Circuit Court is legally recognized — a separate civil wedding is optional, not required.

### Common follow-ups
- "Do we also need a civil wedding?" (Not legally, under PD 1083 — but some couples choose one.)
- "What if my father can't be my wali?" (The role passes to the nearest adult male relative; confirm with your imam.)
- "Can a woman be a witness?" (Practice varies by school — confirm with your imam.)

### Caveats / what NOT to say
- Don't prescribe one school's ruling as "the" rule — Filipino Muslims are mostly Shafi'i, but practice varies; defer to the couple's imam.
- Don't treat the Muslim ceremony as needing civil validation to "count" — PD 1083 makes it legal on its own.
- Don't dismiss any pillar as optional — all five are required for validity.

## Mahr — the bridal gift

**Tags:** mahr, dowry, muslim, bridal-gift, budget
**Applies to:** Muslim couples
**Cross-ref:** Muslim_Wedding_Spec_2026-06-28.md, 08_Budget_Allocation_Reference.md
**Source:** Qur'an 4:4 · prophetic tradition on simplicity · NCMF
**Last verified:** 2026-06-28 · Setnayan team (pending imam review)

The mahr is a **mandatory gift from the groom to the bride** and belongs to her alone — not to her family. It can be money, gold, property, or something symbolic of genuine value to her, and it can be given in full at the wedding (prompt) or partly deferred. Islam praises **simplicity** — the most blessed marriages are those of least burden — so an extravagant mahr is discouraged. In Setnayan, the mahr appears as a budget line marked as a gift to the bride; it is never a Setnayan or vendor charge, and Setnayan never processes it.

### Common follow-ups
- "How much should the mahr be?" (Whatever the couple/families agree — simplicity is encouraged; there's no fixed amount.)
- "Can the mahr be non-cash?" (Yes — gold, property, or something meaningful.)
- "Is the mahr the same as the dowry her family gets?" (No — the mahr is the bride's own, not her family's.)

### Caveats / what NOT to say
- Never push a larger mahr — simplicity is the prophetic ideal.
- Don't confuse mahr with a platform cost or vendor payment.
- Don't imply the family receives the mahr.

## The Walima — the wedding feast

**Tags:** walima, feast, reception, muslim, halal, gender-separation
**Applies to:** Muslim couples
**Cross-ref:** Muslim_Wedding_Spec_2026-06-28.md, Catering_Dietary_Halal_Model_2026-06-11.md
**Source:** prophetic tradition (Sunnah of walima) · NCMF · Setnayan vendor data
**Last verified:** 2026-06-28 · Setnayan team (pending imam review)

The walima is the public wedding feast that announces and celebrates the marriage — a strongly recommended Sunnah. It's the Muslim wedding's "reception," with three overlays: it should be **announced publicly** (the whole point is to make the marriage known); it should favor **simplicity over extravagance**; and it must be a **halal celebration** — no alcohol or pork, modest entertainment, and, depending on the family, separate areas for men and women. Setnayan applies a halal filter to catering and beverages automatically for Muslim events, offers an optional gender-separated seating mode, and surfaces ethno-cultural music and décor per the couple's sub-type.

### Common follow-ups
- "Do we have to separate men and women?" (It depends on your family's practice — Setnayan makes it an option you control.)
- "Can we serve alcohol for non-Muslim guests?" (A walima is a halal celebration; Setnayan routes you to alcohol-free bar options.)
- "Is the walima the same day as the nikah?" (It can be the same day or separate — both are common.)

### Caveats / what NOT to say
- Don't assume gender separation — many Filipino-Muslim weddings are mixed; let the couple decide.
- Don't upsell extravagance — simplicity is praised.
- Don't recommend alcohol or non-halal catering.

## Despedida de soltera

**Tags:** despedida, pre-wedding, parties, traditions, bridal-shower
**Applies to:** all couples (Filipino tradition; co-ed variants common)
**Cross-ref:** 04_Planning_Timelines.md
**Source:** Filipino cultural practice · Setnayan internal observations
**Last verified:** 2026-05-18 · Setnayan team

Despedida de soltera ("farewell of the unmarried woman") is the traditional Filipino bridal shower or send-off party for the bride. Held in the weeks before the wedding, attended by the bride's female family + close friends + the wedding entourage. Modern variants include:

- **Traditional all-female despedida** — bridal shower with games, gifts, and a focus on the bride
- **Co-ed despedida** — both the bride and groom celebrate with mixed friends and family (more common in NCR / younger couples)
- **Despedida-cum-bridal-shower hybrid** — Western bridal shower with Filipino name; lots of games, surprise reveals
- **Bachelorette / bachelor party** — Western-style send-off parties, sometimes paired with traditional despedida

There's no rigid timing — usually within 2-4 weeks of the wedding. Gifts typically lean toward household items for the new couple's home.

### Common follow-ups
- "Do we have to do a despedida?"
- "Can the groom attend the bride's despedida?"

### Caveats / what NOT to say
- Don't prescribe a specific format — couples adapt this freely.

## Engagement length expectations

**Tags:** engagement, length, timeline, expectations, planning-window
**Applies to:** all couples
**Cross-ref:** 04_Planning_Timelines.md
**Source:** Setnayan internal data · Filipino wedding industry observation
**Last verified:** 2026-05-18 · Setnayan team

Typical Filipino engagement lengths:

- **12-18 months** — most common for full weddings with church + reception. Gives time for vendor lock, Pre-Cana scheduling, marriage license, and venue inventory at peak dates.
- **6-12 months** — feasible but compressed. Some categories (custom gown, peak-Saturday photographers) may be limited.
- **< 6 months** — possible but requires aggressive parallel planning. Setnayan AI's compressed-timeline mode handles this.
- **> 18 months** — common when couples are saving up or coordinating family logistics. Setnayan AI is a ₱499/28-day-cycle subscription that simply keeps running until your wedding date and ends right after, so a long engagement just means more cycles — there's no access cap to watch.

Long engagements are sometimes pragmatic (couples accumulating savings, waiting for visa processing, coordinating with overseas relatives' availability). Short engagements are sometimes situational (pregnancy, relative's health, military deployment).

### Common follow-ups
- "Is 6 months enough time?"
- "Is a 2-year engagement too long?"

### Caveats / what NOT to say
- Don't judge engagement length — it's deeply personal.
