Setnayan · naming study

Making the line control obvious

Four ways to name the same thing. The test: a caterer who has never seen this should understand it with no explanation at all.

Why the current one is confusing

AlwaysAn adverb. Always what? Always included, presumably — but it doesn’t say so.
SwapActively wrong. Nothing is swapped. The couple removes it and gets credit. This is the worst offender.
Pick oneFine on its own — but it’s an instruction to the couple, sitting beside words aimed at the vendor.
Add moreReads like a button that adds a row to the form, not a description of the line.

Three grammatical shapes, two different audiences, one wrong word. That’s the confusion — not the concept.

Option A

Say what the couple can do

Every label finishes the same sentence: “The couple…”. One voice, one audience, and “remove” replaces the wrong word.

Trade-offLongest labels — tight on a phone. But it is unambiguous with zero explanation.
Option B

Four plain nouns

Each label names what kind of line this is. Consistent part of speech, very short, scannable down a long list.

Trade-off“Optional” and “Choices” are clear; “Extras” is slightly abstract until you see the price field.
Option C · recommended

Two groups instead of four buttons

The real insight: “Add more” was never the same kind of thing. The first three describe something already inside the package. An extra hour is not in the package — it’s something they can bolt on. Splitting them removes the odd one out, and each group then has an obvious three- or two-way choice.

What’s in the package
What they can add on
Why this oneThe vendor already thinks this way — “here’s what’s included, here’s what costs extra.” It also makes the couple’s screen honest: included lines and add-ons genuinely behave differently.
Option D

A sentence, in a dropdown

No cramming. Full sentences, room to breathe, impossible to misread. Costs one extra tap per line.

Trade-offSlowest to author and you can’t see all options at once — but the least explaining of any option here.
My recommendation: C, with C’s labels borrowed from A. Splitting “add-ons” out of the four-way control fixes the real problem — it was never the same kind of choice. Then within “what’s in the package”, three plain options (Always included · They can remove it · They choose one) need no explanation at all.
