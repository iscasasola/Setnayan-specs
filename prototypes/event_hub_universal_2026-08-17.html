One design. Four very different events. The structure never moves — the words, the name treatment and the list of rooms change to fit what is actually happening. Below: what is expected, then what has to change to get there.

02

Getting from one room to another

You said you had not verified this and asked me to drop it if it turned out to be false. It is true, and worse than you put it. I read every link out of all thirteen rooms.

What happens today

Not one room links to any other room. The bottom bar exists only on the event page — none of the other twelve carry it.

seat → back to the event page, only find your seat→ back to the event page, only find my table → back to the event page, only walk the room → back to the event page, only send a gift → back to the event page, only the story → back to the event page, only ───────────────────────────────────────── welcome → nothing ask to join → nothing photo wall → nothing printable → nothing

A guest standing in the venue looking at their seat has to go back to the event page and find their way again — and four rooms give them no way out at all.

What is expected

The contents page, printed under the masthead. Not a hidden sheet behind a button — a magazine tells you what is inside on the way in. It lists only what this event actually has, and says plainly when something isn’t open yet.

The bar travels. Every room carries the same five buttons, so “back” is never the only way out.

Each room ends with the contents again, so the guest lands somewhere useful instead of reversing.

Nothing here adds a sixth button. The bar stays at five — that decision is right and I am not reopening it.

The contents page is drawn into all four phones above. A row that isn’t open yet stays visible and greyed with its reason (“on the day”) — but a row for something the host has kept private is not drawn at all, because a greyed-out one would announce that photos exist and are being held back.

05

What can be improved — the delta from today

Itemised, each with the reason it is on the list. Ordered by what a person would notice first.

01

The words follow the event the main job

A guest at a graduation reads “the couple” sixty-eight times across the Hub — fifty-four of them on the event page alone. Every kind of event already has its own words written down and waiting; the page has simply never asked for them.

Why: it is not a taste problem, it is a word count — and eight in ten of those words sit on one page, so most of the value lands in one pass.

02

A name treatment for each kind of event design

Four grammars, one spine: two names with an ampersand (wedding, unchanged); one honoured name with an occasion line (birthday, debut, graduation); an organisation above a titled occasion (corporate, gala); a name with two dates (a wake).

Why: today the opening splits on the word “and”. A gala called “Design & Build Summit” would be stacked across two lines with a wedding’s italic gold ampersand between the halves of its own name.

03

The wedding-only parts learn to stay home the biggest build

Five things belong to weddings and should keep every wedding word they have: the two-name opening, the love story, the bride’s and groom’s sides, the five cinematic openings, the tea ceremony. They must simply never appear elsewhere.

Why: there is no way to do that today. The page asks the event type one question — may this event have a page at all? — and nothing else. So a birthday booked far enough ahead would be handed the wedding save-the-date machinery, and a company summit’s opening would get a lettered wedding monogram. This is the largest piece of work in the plan and nothing else is blocked on it.

04

A contents page under the masthead navigation

One printed list of what is in this event, resolved by the same rules as the bottom bar, showing only what this event actually has.

Why: measured — no room links to any other room, the bar exists on only one of the thirteen, and four rooms have no way out at all. A guest at their table cannot reach the directions, the gifts or the album without starting over.

05

The bar travels with the guest navigation

The same five buttons on every room. Still five — no sixth button, ever.

Why: the rooms were each given their own hand-copied top strip. One of them says so in its own notes.

06

Look at the redesign that already shipped costs nothing

Seven pieces of work in July rebuilt this page’s whole appearance and merged. Its hand-over note asks whoever picks it up to look at it on a phone first. Nobody has.

Why: two small wording decisions are still waiting on you, and it is possible some of what looks unfinished is simply unreviewed.

07

Decide the grid above yours alone

Which rooms exist for which kind of event.

Why: it decides how much of the rest is a design problem at all. Four cells I could not call for you.

Nothing here has been validated against a real customer, and it cannot be. Every event in production today is a wedding — six events, thirty-nine guests, fourteen photos, no orders ever placed. So the four screens above are a claim about the design, not an observation of it working. The wedding column is the only one anyone has ever seen.
